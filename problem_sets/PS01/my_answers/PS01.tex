	\section*{Question 1: Education}

A school counselor was curious about the average of IQ of the students in her school and took a random sample of 25 students' IQ scores. The following is the data set:\\
\vspace{.5cm}

\lstinputlisting[language=R, firstline=39, lastline=39]{PS01.R}  

\vspace{1cm}

\begin{enumerate}
	\item Find a 90\% confidence interval for the average student IQ in the school (As a hint, you first need the mean and SD to create a CI):\\
	
	\lstinputlisting[language=R, firstline=40, lastline=80]{PS01.R}  
	
	\noindent \textbf{Answer:} The 90\% confidence interval for the average student IQ is $[93.96,\ 102.92]$.
	
\noindent A confidence interval is the sample mean plus or minus a margin of error, where the margin of error is a critical value times the standard error:
\[ \bar{y} \pm t_{1-\alpha/2,\, n-1} \times \frac{s}{\sqrt{n}} \]

\noindent I use the t-distribution rather than the normal (z) distribution mainly because the sample is small. With only 25 students, the sample standard deviation is an uncertain estimate of the population standard deviation, which is unknown. The t-distribution accounts for this extra uncertainty with thicker tails, which gives a slightly wider, more cautious interval. With a large sample, the two distributions would give almost the same result.

\noindent The sample has $n = 25$, a mean of $\bar{y} = 98.44$, and a standard deviation of $s = 13.09$. For a 90\% interval, $\alpha = 0.10$, so 5\% is left in each tail, with $df = n - 1 = 24$:
\[ SE = \frac{13.09}{\sqrt{25}} = 2.619 \qquad t_{0.95,\,24} = 1.711 \]
\[ 98.44 \pm 1.711 \times 2.619 = 98.44 \pm 4.48 = [93.96,\ 102.92] \]
The built-in \texttt{t.test()} function gives the same interval.

\noindent This means I am 90\% confident that the true mean IQ of students at this school lies between 93.96 and 102.92. In other words, if we repeatedly took random samples of 25 students and built an interval this way each time, about 90\% of those intervals would contain the true mean. The national average of 100 falls inside this interval, so the sample is consistent with the school being about average.

	\item Next, the school counselor was curious  whether  the average student IQ in her school is higher than the average IQ score (100) among all the schools in the country.\\ 
	
	\noindent Using the same sample, conduct the appropriate hypothesis test with $\alpha=0.05$.
	
	\lstinputlisting[language=R, firstline=82, lastline=101]{PS01.R}  
	
	\noindent \textbf{Answer:} No. There is not enough evidence at $\alpha = 0.05$ to conclude that the school's average IQ is higher than 100.
	
	\noindent I use a one-sided test because the counselor's question has a specific direction: whether the school's average is \textit{higher} than 100, not just different from it. The hypotheses are:
	\[ H_0: \mu \le 100 \qquad H_A: \mu > 100 \]
	I use a t-test for the same reason as in part 1: the sample is small and the population standard deviation has to be estimated from it.
	
	\noindent The test statistic measures how many standard errors the sample mean is from 100:
	\[ t = \frac{\bar{y} - \mu_0}{SE} = \frac{98.44 - 100}{2.619} = -0.596 \]
	The test is calculated at $\mu_0 = 100$ because it is the edge of $H_0$: if we cannot reject at 100, we cannot reject for any value below it either. Because $H_A$ is $\mu > 100$, the p-value is the area in the upper tail of the t-distribution with 24 degrees of freedom (\texttt{lower.tail = FALSE}):
	\[ p = P(T_{24} \ge -0.596) = 0.722 \]
	The built-in \texttt{t.test()} function with \texttt{alternative = "greater"} gives the same result.
	
	\noindent Since $p = 0.722 > \alpha = 0.05$, I fail to reject the null hypothesis. This is not surprising. The sample mean (98.44) is actually below 100, so the data do not point in the direction of the alternative hypothesis at all.
	
\end{enumerate}
